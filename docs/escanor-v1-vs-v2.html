Part 1

How v1 worked

v1 asked one question per game: whose offense looks better compared with what the other defense gives up? It answered with 14 ratios per team.

ratio = offense's stat ÷ opposing defense's allowed stat (3rd/4th down: offense conv% ÷ defense stop%)

A ratio of 1.0 means the offense matches what that defense usually allows. Each ratio was multiplied by a hand-picked weight, the four categories were weighted 33 / 22 / 18 / 27, and the team with the bigger total was the pick. The gap between totals was the Confidence Factor (CF).

Where v1's weight went

Each stat's share of a team's total. 3rd-down conversion alone was over a fifth of the model.

PassingRushingReceivingDowns

The four problems hiding in it

  • Backwards defense comparison. Dividing by what a defense allows makes an offense look worse against a bad defense.
  • Raw counts. TDs, sacks and rush yards were season totals, so a team with one more game played looked better, and a team coming off a bye looked worse.
  • Tiny samples and zeros. A defense that allowed 6 of 7 fourth downs, or 0 TDs, produced ratios of 3, 5 or more that could decide a game by themselves.
  • No spread logic. It picked winners, then glued the line on. A 1-point favorite and a 14-point favorite were treated the same way.
Part 2

How v2 works

Step 1: build each team's rates

v2 reads every finished game's box score from ESPN (completions, attempts, yards, TDs, sacks, 3rd/4th downs, drives, turnovers) and adds them up per team, for offense and for what opponents did against them. Because it knows games played, every stat becomes a rate: TDs per play, sacks per dropback, rush yards per game, turnovers per drive.

Early in the season those rates rest on one or two games, so v2 mixes in last season's stats, counted as if they were 2 games. The backtest tested 0, 2, 4 and 8 and found 2 fit best. Last season's share fades automatically as games pile up:

After 1 game, two thirds of a team's numbers come from last season. By Week 10 it is under 20%.

Each rate is also smoothed: it gets one average game's worth of league-average numbers mixed in. That's why a team with 0 TDs allowed reads as "a bit better than average," not "infinitely good."

Step 2: size up each matchup

index = (offense rate ÷ league average) × (defense allowed rate ÷ league average)

Both halves now point the same way. A strong offense and a leaky defense both push the index up. For sacks and turnovers, where a higher number is bad for the offense, the index is flipped. Every index is capped between 0.5 and 2.0 so no single stat can run away with a game.

Example: a 107.5 passer rating (league average ≈ 92)

v1 rated the easy matchup lower than the hard one. v2 rates it higher, which is how football actually works.

Step 3: turn indexes into points

v2 subtracts the away team's index from the home team's for each stat, multiplies by a weight, adds them up, and adds +2.0 points for home field. The weights were fitted with a regression on 2024–2025 results, so the output is a projected final margin in points.

Hand-picked (v1) vs fitted (v2): share of total weight

v1 hand-pickedv2 fitted from results

The data disagreed with the old instincts. 3rd-down conversion fell from 21% to 2%, because once passing efficiency is in, 3rd-down success adds little. Yards per attempt, 4th-down conversion and rushing TD rate rose to the top. Turnovers, which v1 never used, earned about 6%. One weight (Rec 1st-down %) came out slightly negative, which is noise rather than a real effect.

Step 4: pick against the line

v2 writes its projection as a betting line and compares it with the real one. The difference is the edge, and the pick is the side the edge favors.

Example from 2025 Week 6: Vegas had the Falcons as 3.5-point home underdogs. v2 thought they were only 1.9-point underdogs. The Falcons are 1.6 points better than the line gives them credit for, so the pick is Falcons +3.5 with an edge of 1.6.

This is why v2's confidence numbers are small (usually 0–4). They're in points, and the closing line is rarely far from what a stats model thinks.

What went wrong in v1

Two ratios decided it. 4th downs (−20.3): the Falcons defense had allowed 6 of 7, so its "stop rate" was 14% and the Bills' ratio came out 5.25. Rushing TDs (−13.0): 9 Bills TDs ÷ 2 allowed = 4.5, a raw count against a tiny denominator. Neither says much about the Falcons.

v2 Stat-by-stat pull (points)

← favors Billsfavors Falcons →
Stats: −3.9Home field: +2.0Projected: Bills by 1.9Pick: Falcons +3.5, edge 1.6

Result: Falcons 24, Bills 14

v2's pick covered, v1's lost. The same 4th-down stat is still there, but smoothing and the cap shrink it to −1.8 points. The 2024 blend adds context, and home field is counted. This is one game, chosen because it shows the mechanics clearly. Over 511 games, the two versions beat the spread equally often.

Why the spread number didn't move

The closing line is set by a market that already knows everything in a box score, plus injuries, weather, QB news and sharp money. A model built only from box scores can get very good at predicting games and still only re-discover the line. The backtest checked this directly: after accounting for the line, v2's projection added no measurable information about the final margin.

Put simply, if v2 were as accurate as Vegas, it would still hit about 50% against Vegas, because it would just be copying the line.

How much luck is in these numbers

With 511 games, a true 50% model lands anywhere from about 45.7% to 54.3% just by chance. Your live 2025-26 record (45.1%) and both backtests (49–50%) are all consistent with a coin flip. Top-5 records use only 170 picks, so their range is about ±7.5 points.

What to expect from v2

Early-season picksMore stable than v1. Last season's data keeps Week 2–4 numbers from swinging on one game.
The confidence numberNow means something concrete (points of disagreement with the line). In the backtest, the top-5 edges went 51.8%, only slightly above the rest and well within luck. Treat it as a ranking, not a probability.

What could still make it better

  • Information the line reacts to late: injuries (especially QB), weather and rest days. This is the most promising direction, because it's what a box score can't see.
  • Earlier lines: grade against opening lines instead of closing lines. Models sometimes beat the opener before the market corrects.
  • Pass only when the edge is big: worth testing with more seasons of data; two seasons aren't enough to trust a threshold.
  • More history: each extra season shrinks the luck range and makes the fitted weights steadier. Run python fetch_history.py 2026 and python backtest.py after the season.

Switching and re-checking

  • Which model runs: MODEL_VERSION in config.py. "v2" is the default; "v1" brings back the original.
  • Re-run the comparison: python backtest.py prints the stage table above and refits v2's settings into data\escanor_v2_params.json.
  • Where the code lives: v1 in predictor.py (unchanged), v2 in escanor_v2.py, data loading in espn_data.py.

Correction

During the build I said Fix 2 converts the score gap at "about 2.1 points per unit." The fitted value was actually about 0.2–0.3 points per unit (a gap of 10 ≈ 2–3 points). The backtest numbers above were always computed with the correct value.
